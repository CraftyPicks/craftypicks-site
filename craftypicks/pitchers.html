The bars are strikeout totals, not a trend. Every past start is scored against tonight's line, not the number that was posted on the night, so a 6–4 record doesn't mean a pitcher is hot — it means six of his last ten outings would have cleared the number a book is offering today. Ten starts is a small sample: the distance between 6–4 and 4–6 is two ordinary nights, and the record on its own is not a reason to bet anything. A card flagged in amber sits further from the posted line than the market can plausibly be wrong, which we treat as our bug rather than an opportunity.

What the projection is

One line of arithmetic, published so it can be wrong

The formula

Rate × workload × matchup

Season strikeouts per nine innings, times the innings a starter is expected to go, adjusted for how often tonight's lineup strikes out relative to the other teams on the board. That is the entire model. No fitted coefficients, nothing that couldn't be checked on paper.

The fragile part

The innings assumption

Expected innings does more work here than anything else. Assume six innings instead of five and a third, and every projection on the page moves half a strikeout — enough to invent disagreements that aren't there. It's a flat assumption by starter type rather than a per-pitcher estimate, and it is the first thing to suspect when a number looks wrong.

Left out

Park, weather, catcher, umpire

All of them nudge strikeout totals. None nudge it as far as which lineup he faces and how long he stays in, and every additional input is another thing that can be quietly wrong without anyone noticing for a month.

Shown, not counted

The vs-opponent line

How a starter has fared against tonight's club is on every card because it's the first thing anyone looks for. It is not in the projection. A handful of starts against one team is mostly luck, which is why any sample under three starts is greyed out and labelled thin.

A rating is not a bet. Most of these sit close enough to the posted number that there is nothing to do. Only the starters that clear the screens get posted as plays, and those are capped at five a day — the rest are here so the arithmetic can be checked in public.
