The second system

The strikeout screens

Craftypicks runs two methods that disagree with each other on purpose. The scanner hunts for prices the market has wrong. These screens ignore price almost entirely and look for a situation instead: the right arm against the right lineup. Both post to the same card, both get graded in public, and the track record reports them separately so you can see which one is actually working.

Everything on this page is generated from the configuration file the scanner reads. The thresholds below aren't a description of the rules — they are the rules, pulled from the same values that ran this morning. If a number changes in the code, it changes here on the next build. A published methodology that quietly drifts from the code is worse than publishing nothing.

Screen B

Elite arm at 6.5 — plus money only

The same idea one rung higher: a genuine high-volume strikeout arm at a number most pitchers can't reach. This screen hits close to a coin flip, and that single fact decides everything about it — at −120 a coin flip loses money, at +110 it makes money. So the price gate isn't a preference here, it's the rule that makes the screen viable at all.

The K/9 requirement exists to separate a real strikeout pitcher from an inflated rate. A ground-ball starter who works five innings can post a strong K% without ever approaching seven strikeouts — the rate says he misses bats when he's out there, and the volume says he isn't out there long enough for it to matter.

Screen C

The under — and the arms it refuses

A capable but not overpowering starter against a lineup that doesn't strike out. The important rule here is the exclusion: high-strikeout arms are thrown out of this screen entirely rather than merely discouraged. They beat weak-contact lineups on pure stuff, and betting the under on them has lost repeatedly.

What we don't know yet

The part most sites leave out

Unproven

These screens have not been validated

They're a set of rules, running live, with every play graded in public from day one. That's the test. Nobody has shown they beat the closing number over a meaningful sample, including us — and until the record says otherwise, that's exactly how they should be treated.

Suspect

The vs-roster data is the weakest input

Screens A and B require a career line against the opposing roster. Those samples are small, they mix seasons where the pitcher was a different pitcher, and they skew toward veterans facing division rivals — which is a filter on the schedule as much as on the matchup. Screen C uses none of it, so comparing C against A and B over time is itself a test of whether that input earns its place.

Ignored

The screens barely look at price

Beyond the juice caps, a screen play can be posted at a number the wider market says is poor value. That's deliberate — the claim being tested is that the situation is worth more than the price implies. It's also the most likely way this system loses money, and the "vs close" column is what will catch it.

Limited

Coverage is a handful of games a day

Prop odds are billed one game at a time, so the screens only see the games we already pull props for. They are not scanning the full slate, and a play they'd love could go unexamined simply because it wasn't among the games checked.
