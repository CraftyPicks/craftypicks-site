The second system

The strikeout screens

Craftypicks runs two methods that disagree with each other on purpose. The scanner hunts for prices the market has wrong. These screens ignore price almost entirely and look for a situation instead: the right arm against the right lineup. Both post to the same card, both get graded in public, and the track record reports them separately so you can see which one is actually working.

Everything on this page is generated from the configuration file the scanner reads. The thresholds below aren't a description of the rules — they are the rules, pulled from the same values that ran this morning. If a number changes in the code, it changes here on the next build. A published methodology that quietly drifts from the code is worse than publishing nothing.

Screen A

Standard over — the proven core

A strikeout pitcher against a lineup that strikes out, at a number low enough to clear on an ordinary start rather than a special one. This is the screen the system trusts most, which is why it carries the tightest daily limit — a good filter applied to too many games stops being a filter.

Screen B

Elite arm at the higher number

The same idea one rung higher: a genuine high-volume strikeout arm at a number most pitchers can't reach. This screen hits close to a coin flip, and that single fact decides everything about it — at −120 a coin flip loses money, at +110 it makes money.

There is no price requirement on this screen. Selecting a matchup and judging a price are separate questions, and a rule that demanded plus money was answering the second one with a coin-flip heuristic — a good spot at −115 was rejected while a bad one at +105 was not. Every screen play still gets its edge measured against the vig-free consensus and its closing line recorded, which is the honest version of that check.

The K/9 requirement exists to separate a real strikeout pitcher from an inflated rate. A ground-ball starter who works five innings can post a strong K% without ever approaching seven strikeouts — the rate says he misses bats when he's out there, and the volume says he isn't out there long enough for it to matter.

Universal rules

Rules that apply to every screen

These caps are currently switched off. Every one of them — the ceiling on high numbers, the ban on 4.5 lines, and the limit on how much juice is acceptable — has been lifted, so a play is judged on the screen conditions alone. That widens the pool considerably and removes protections that were there for a reason; the track record will show whether lifting them was right.

An earlier version capped each screen separately, which meant pitchers rejected by Screen A's limit simply reappeared under Screen B — five qualifiers produced five bets from a cap of two. A cap another screen can walk around isn't a cap.

This table is the whole argument for the juice caps. A screen that wins 55% of the time is excellent at −110 and merely fine at −150. Paying up erases the edge quietly, without ever showing up as a losing day — you just keep winning and slowly not making money.

What we don't know yet

The part most sites leave out

Unproven

These screens have not been validated

They're a set of rules, running live, with every play graded in public from day one. That's the test. Nobody has shown they beat the closing number over a meaningful sample, including us — and until the record says otherwise, that's exactly how they should be treated.

Suspect

The vs-roster data is the weakest input

Both screens require a career line against the opposing roster. Those samples are small, they mix seasons where the pitcher was a different pitcher, and they skew toward veterans facing division rivals — which is a filter on the schedule as much as on the matchup. At a few dozen plate appearances the error bar on a batting average is wider than the thresholds being applied to it, so this block rejects on noise as often as on signal. It is the first thing that should go if the screens stay too quiet.

Ignored

The screens barely look at price

Beyond the juice caps, a screen play can be posted at a number the wider market says is poor value. That's deliberate — the claim being tested is that the situation is worth more than the price implies. It's also the most likely way this system loses money, and the "vs close" column is what will catch it.

Limited

Coverage is a handful of games a day

Prop odds are billed one game at a time, so the screens only see the games we already pull props for. They are not scanning the full slate, and a play they'd love could go unexamined simply because it wasn't among the games checked.
